# Research Notes: Bronze Age Settlements and Archaeological Discoveries in Dubai

**Compiled:** September 2026  
**Subject:** Bronze Age Archaeological Settlements, Lithic-Metallurgical Transitions, and Geophysical Discoveries in Dubai, UAE  
**Curated for:** Digital Humanities & Arabian Peninsula Archaeology Research Compendium  

---

## 1. Executive Summary

Over the past few decades, and particularly through intensive excavations and advanced geophysical surveys conducted between 2020 and 2025/2026, the Emirate of Dubai has emerged as a cornerstone for understanding Bronze Age human settlement, pastoral mobility, technology, and funerary architecture in southeastern Arabia (the ancient region of **Magan**). 

Spanning the **Hafit Period (ca. 3200–2500 BCE)**, the **Umm Al-Nar Period (ca. 2500–2000 BCE)**, and the **Wadi Suq / Late Bronze Age (ca. 2000–1300 BCE)**, Dubai's archaeological landscape demonstrates that rather than being an inhospitable desert periphery, the area supported:
- Highly connected coastal trading communities (e.g., **Al Sufouh**).
- Deep inland seasonal encampments and specialized hunting stations (e.g., **Al Ashoush**, **Saruq Al-Hadid** Horizon V/IV).
- Extensive multi-period necropolises linking coastal dunes to the Hajar Mountains (e.g., **Al Qusais**, **Jabal Al Yamh** in Hatta, **Margham**).
- A remarkable technological symbiosis where high-skilled stone microlith production co-existed alongside early bronze/copper metallurgy.
- Non-invasive 21st-century geophysical methods (GPR, magnetometry) revealing hundreds of buried subsurface structures.

## 2. Chronological Framework for the UAE Bronze Age

| Period | Approximate Date Range | Regional Characteristics in UAE / Dubai | Key Dubai Sites |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hafit Period** | ca. 3200 – 2500 BCE | Cairn/beehive above-ground tombs; early trade with Jemdet Nasr Mesopotamia; pastoral nomadic mobility. | Jabal Al Yamh (Hatta), Margham (early forms) |
| **Umm Al-Nar Period** | ca. 2500 – 2000 BCE | Circular communal ashlar tombs; intensive copper extraction and trade (Magan); oasis agriculture; maritime trade with Indus & Mesopotamia. | Al Sufouh, Al Ashoush, Saruq Al-Hadid (Horizon V base) |
| **Wadi Suq Period** | ca. 2000 – 1600 BCE | Climatic aridification; subterranean cist & long communal graves; shift in ceramic forms (painted cups, string-cut bases); hunting-pastoralism. | Saruq Al-Hadid (Horizon IV), Al Qusais, Jabal Al Yamh |
| **Late Bronze Age (LBA)** | ca. 1600 – 1300 BCE | Continued adaptation to aridification; soft-stone carving; coexistence of stone armatures and bronze weapons; prelude to Iron Age metallurgy. | Saruq Al-Hadid (Horizon IV upper), Al Qusais, Margham |
| **Iron Age Transition** | ca. 1300 – 300 BCE | Falaj irrigation; massive copper/bronze smelting industrial complexes; snake cult rituals. | Saruq Al-Hadid (Industrial Horizon), Al Qusais Mound of Serpents |

## 3. Detailed Site Analyses in the Emirate of Dubai

### 3.1. Saruq Al-Hadid (ساروق الحديد)

* **Location:** Southern desert of Dubai, on the northern edge of the Rub' al-Khali desert (24°40' N, 55°14' E).
* **Discovery:** 2002 by HH Sheikh Mohammed bin Rashid Al Maktoum while flying over desert dunes.
* **Excavation Projects:** Dubai Desert Survey, Dubai Municipality, SHARP (Saruq al-Hadid Archaeological Research Project / University of New England Australia, 2014–2017), Sanisera Archaeological Institute, and Dubai Culture (2020–present).

#### Bronze Age Stratigraphy & Chronology
While internationally celebrated for its monumental Iron Age metallurgical workshop (yielding tons of copper slag and thousands of bronze ritual snakes, swords, and gold ornaments), recent stratigraphic analyses have demonstrated critical Bronze Age horizons:
1. **Horizon V (ca. 2000–1750 BCE):** Pit features and post holes excavated directly into the underlying gypsum pavement, spanning the transition from terminal Umm an-Nar into early Wadi Suq.
2. **Horizon IV (ca. 1750–1300 BCE – Wadi Suq to Late Bronze Age):** 
   - A dense, stratified bone midden deposit measuring over 750 square metres and up to 1 metre in thickness.
   - Contains over **one metric ton of animal bone fragments**, dominated by wild desert fauna (Arabian oryx, gazelle, and dromedary camel), hearths, ceramics, marine shell, and soft-stone vessels.
   - Yielded **14 copper-based arrowheads** of mid-second millennium BCE type in its upper layers.

#### Scholarly Focus: Bronze Age Stone Flaking & Microlith Technology (Moore et al. 2022)
Excavations published in *PLOS ONE* (Moore, Weeks, Cable, Al-Ali, Boraik, Zein, 2022) revealed an assemblage of approximately **9,100 stone artefacts** from Horizon IV:
- **Raw Material:** Fine-grained chert cobbles imported from wadi gravel sources outside the local dune field.
- **Core Reduction Technique:** Ad-hoc, low-cost hard-hammer percussion without rigid hierarchical core reduction stages. Cores were frequently rotated to extract usable flakes.
- **Microlith Manufacture:** Flakes were selected and modified using highly controlled backing and truncation retouch to produce standardized, stylized scalene geometric microliths.
- **Weaponry Continuity:** Microliths were mounted as composite projectile armatures for hunting arrows, functioning alongside copper-alloy projectile points. This provides definitive evidence of the persistent economic and technical utility of lithic toolkits long after the advent of metallurgy in the Arabian Peninsula.

#### 2025 Non-Invasive Geophysical Breakthrough (Dubai Culture & Khalifa University)
In **February 2025**, Dubai Culture and Arts Authority, in collaboration with researchers from the Khalifa University of Science and Technology, deployed a state-of-the-art non-invasive geophysical survey:
- **Instruments:** High-resolution Ground-Penetrating Radar (GPR), caesium vapor magnetic gradiometry, and high-precision centimeter-accurate topographic mapping.
- **Key Findings:**
  - Detection and spatial localisation of **over 400 subterranean anomalies** (metallic clusters, kilns, and architectural alignments).
  - Identification of **5 principal buried architectural structures** concealed under deep sand dunes.
  - Delineation of **6 high-potential archaeological zones** across the primary site.
  - Achieved a **30% reduction** in excavation exploration time and operational costs while preserving delicate cultural layers from unnecessary disturbance.

### 3.2. Al Sufouh Archaeological Site (موقع الصفوح الأثري)

* **Location:** Coastal district of Al Sufouh, Dubai (25°06'42" N, 55°10'32" E).
* **Discovery & Excavation:** Discovered in 1988; intensively excavated between 1994 and 1995 by J.N. Benton under the auspices of Dubai Antiquities.
* **Period:** Umm Al-Nar Culture (ca. 2500–2000 BCE; 3rd millennium BCE).

#### Architectural & Funerary Features
- **Circular Ashlar Tomb:** The centerpiece of the site is a classic Umm Al-Nar circular monumental tomb measuring **6.5 metres in diameter**.
- **Construction Masonry:** Built using an inner wall of unworked limestone and coral aggregate, faced externally by a ring wall of finely masoned, dressed curved ashlar stone blocks.
- **Internal Layout:** Dual entrances aligned on a northeast-southwest axis. An internal dividing spine wall bisects the tomb into two equal halves, each subdivided into three distinct burial chambers (six chambers in total).
- **Burial Practices:** Skeletons interred in contracted/flexed positions, typical of collective 3rd-millennium Magan funerary traditions.

#### Material Culture & Occupational Debris
- Ceramic vessels belonging to the Umm Al-Nar painted tradition (fine orange/red ware painted with black geometric motifs and wavy lines).
- Cast copper/bronze daggers, spear tips, and chisels.
- Soft-stone (chlorite/steatite) vessels decorated with dot-in-double-circle incisions.
- Thousands of beads crafted from marine shell, chlorite, and imported carnelian.
- Cylinder seals reflecting direct mercantile interaction with Mesopotamia and Elam.
- Extensive occupational middens surrounding the tomb, rich in burnt ash, fire-cracked rock, teleost fish bones, marine mollusc shells (*Terebralia palustris*, pearl oysters), and mammalian fauna, indicating sustained coastal exploitation.

### 3.3. Al Qusais Archaeological Site & Necropolis (موقع القصيص الأثري)

* **Location:** Al Qusais industrial/residential sector, northeastern Dubai.
* **Discovery & Excavations:** Discovered in 1974; salvage excavations during the 1970s and 1990s uncovered extensive cemetery sectors. Reassessed and scientifically re-excavated in 2020 by the Sanisera Archaeological Institute and Dubai Municipality (Valente et al. 2023).
* **Chronological Span:** Late Bronze Age (Wadi Suq / LBA, ca. 1600–1300 BCE) into Iron Age II (ca. 1100–600 BCE).

#### Funerary Architecture and Skeletal Assemblage
- Over **101 burial pits** mapped in the primary necropolis, containing individual and semi-collective interments of approximately **120 individuals**.
- Graves range from simple earth-cut pits to stone-lined cist graves covered with capstones.
- Accompanied by diagnostic Late Bronze Age grave goods: copper daggers, hemispherical soft-stone bowls, and globular ceramic jars.

#### The "Mound of Serpents" & Cultic Continuity
- Adjacent to the cemetery, excavations revealed a elevated sanctuary area known as the **Mound of Serpents**.
- Features a multi-columned hall dating to the early 1st millennium BCE that grew directly out of Late Bronze Age religious foundations.
- Artifacts include snake-embossed pottery and cast copper snake figurines, representing the ritual snake cult prevalent across south-eastern Arabia (paralleling discoveries at Bithnah and Masafi in Fujairah).

### 3.4. Al Ashoush (موقع العشوش الأثري)

* **Location:** Deep inland desert of Dubai, situated approximately 8 km southeast of Saruq Al-Hadid.
* **Period:** Mid-to-late 3rd millennium BCE (Umm Al-Nar Period).
* **Significance:**
  - Identified during the Dubai Desert Survey (Casana, Herrmann, and Qandil, 2009).
  - One of the exceptionally rare 3rd-millennium BC settlements documented deep within the inland hyper-arid desert zone, far removed from coastal fish resources and piedmont alluvial plains.
  - Represents temporary seasonal pastoral camps, flint-knapping workshops, and hunting camps where mobile groups grazed herds during seasonal humid phases and hunted wild ungulates.

---

### 3.5. Jabal Al Yamh Tombs, Hatta (قبور جبل اليمح - حتا)

* **Location:** Mountainous piedmont and rocky ridges of Hatta, eastern Dubai Emirate.
* **Chronological Span:** Spans continuous funerary use from the Early Bronze Age Hafit Period (ca. 3200–2500 BCE), Umm Al-Nar, Wadi Suq (ca. 2000–1300 BCE), through the Iron Age.
* **Archaeological Survey (Valente et al. 2021/2022):**
  - Systematic survey recorded **84 stone tombs** built along mountain slopes and terraces.
  - Architectural typology includes dry-stone corbelled beehive tombs (Hafit type) and elongated sub-surface cist chambers (Wadi Suq type).
  - Grave goods include incised chlorite vessels, copper alloy finger rings, beads of carnelian, faience, and shell.
  - Dubai Culture has completed the structural restoration of 29 tombs as part of the Hatta Heritage and Cultural Conservation master plan.

---

### 3.6. Margham Archaeological Site (موقع مرغم)

* **Location:** Margham desert district, eastern Dubai.
* **Period:** Late Bronze Age to early Iron Age transition (ca. 1300–600 BCE).
* **Recent Excavations (reported 2024–2025):**
  - Uncovered a semi-circular drystone tomb reflecting architectural echoes of earlier 3rd-millennium traditions.
  - Single primary burial chamber (1.6 m length × 0.96 m width).
  - Funerary equipment included: **10 bronze arrowheads**, a bronze drinking cup, soft-stone bowls, and worked flint cores.

## 4. Key Cross-Cutting Research Themes

### 4.1. Lithic and Metallurgical Coexistence
The empirical evidence from Saruq Al-Hadid Horizon IV (Moore et al. 2022) challenges unilinear technological replacement models. Rather than metal immediately replacing lithics:
- Functional flint/chert armatures remained cost-effective, easily field-sharpened or produced, and ideal for single-use hunting projectiles.
- Copper-base metals were valuable, recyclable prestige assets reserved for combat, status exchange, and specialized hunting gear.

### 4.2. Maritime and Inter-Regional Exchange Networks (Magan)
Dubai's Bronze Age sites formed crucial nodes in regional and intercontinental exchange routes:
- **Marine Resources & Shell Goods:** Coastal settlements like Al Sufouh harvested marine molluscs (*Pinctada*, *Engina mendicaria*, *Terebralia*) used for beads and mother-of-pearl ornaments traded inland to Saruq Al-Hadid and Jabal Al Yamh.
- **Copper Trade:** Dubai sits along transit paths between the copper-rich ophiolite belt of the northern Hajar Mountains and maritime embarkation ports exporting ingots to Mesopotamian cities (Ur, Lagash) and Indus Valley ports (Lothal).
- **Imported Exotic Commodities:** Carnelian from the Indus/Gujarat, chlorite from southeastern Iran or Oman, bitumen from Mesopotamia, and foreign cylinder seals confirm high interregional integration.

### 4.3. Digital Humanities, Remote Sensing, and Non-Invasive Archaeology
The shift from destructive total excavation to precision non-invasive documentation is exemplified by Dubai Culture's recent initiatives:
- Integration of satellite multi-spectral remote sensing to trace palaeo-drainage wadis and vegetation corridors in the Dubai desert.
- Drone-based photogrammetric 3D surface modeling for fragile tomb architecture (Jabal Al Yamh, Al Sufouh).
- Subsurface GPR and magnetometry surveys (Saruq Al-Hadid 2025) creating digital digital twin point clouds of buried archaeological features prior to excavation.
